    ADR-012: Data Governance Catalog, Legal Holds and Enforcement¶

    Status: Proposed Deciders: @grokify Relates to: ADR-011 (lifecycle states, data classification and privacy rights; this ADR defines the catalog, holds and enforcement behind them; it replaces ADR-011's single erasure flag with holds), ADR-007 (roles), ADR-010 (access changes), ADR-013 (key management, which owns snapshot encryption). Updates: a new docs/design/architecture/data-governance.md.

    Context¶

    ADR-011 defines a classification registry, lifecycle states, access and erasure operations, and an erasure hold. It leaves two questions open:

    • What does a hold preserve? A mailbox hold works because email is immutable once sent: "preserve" means "do not delete". A row in a CRUD database is overwritten by an update, and the earlier state is often the evidence. Preserving only against deletion would let the data be altered instead.
    • How is any of it enforced in applications? Each product keeps its own data in its own schema and database. Governance that only covers SystemForge's tables protects little, and routing every product's queries through a SystemForge data service would make the platform the data layer for all of them, contradicting the modular monolith (a product must be able to split out) and adding latency for rare operations. It would not even enforce anything: a holder of a database credential bypasses any interface.

    Governance that spans products is what makes the platform valuable to a product team: classification, privacy rights and holds that work for their data without each team building them. The design below delivers that without taking over their data access.

    Decision¶

    The data catalog¶

    1. The classification registry is a central data catalog. Each registered entity and field is described once, in the model of github.com/grokify/datacatalog (ADR-011, decisions 6 and 7), and the catalog is the answer to "what data do we hold and what rules apply to it". Per entity it records: the owner (product or platform), the data subject key, whether it is subject to access and erasure requests (GDPR scope), whether it can be placed under hold, whether it is versioned, its structured retention (keep, then purge or offload; ADR-011, decision 24) and its eraser. Per field it records the visibility (public or internal), the pii and secret tags, the erasure strategy and the export flag. Per relationship to another registered entity it records the purge disposition (cascade, detach or restrict; ADR-011, decision 21).
    2. The catalog is queryable and published. It is available through an API and a command, and rendered as a documentation page from the same source, so a data protection review, a customer questionnaire and the enforcement code read one definition. Products' entries appear in it when they register (decision 16).

    Legal holds¶

    1. A hold means preserve, and preservation covers alteration as well as deletion. A subject covered by a hold has its data preserved as of the hold and protected from change.
    2. A hold is an object, and a subject may be on several at once. A hold has an identifier, a name, an optional external reference (a matter number that the platform stores and does not interpret), a reason, a state (active or released), its placing and releasing actors, and a set of custodians: the subjects it covers. A subject is held while at least one active hold covers it. Releasing one hold never clears another; the subject becomes erasable only when no active hold remains.
    3. Custodians are subjects, grouped by the hold. A hold lists its custodians explicitly. As a convenience a hold may be placed on an organization, which adds the organization's members at that time and records the hold against the organization. Whether members who join later are also added is chosen when the hold is placed (include_future_members), and defaults to no. The setting is recorded with the hold and changing it is an audited change by the legal manager. The platform understands the grouping (which subjects a hold covers, and which holds cover a subject) and nothing about the matter itself.
    4. Placing a hold snapshots each custodian. For every custodian the platform runs the same Export operation (ADR-011, decision 9) and stores the result as a sealed, immutable artifact with a digest, time, actor and hold. The snapshot answers "what did it say when the hold began". A subject added to a hold later is snapshotted at that moment.
    5. While any hold covers a subject:
    6. erasure, deletion and purge of its records are refused with ErrHeld, and a dependent row covered by a hold blocks the purge of the subject it references (decision 22);
    7. edits to pii fields are refused with ErrHeld, so the snapshot remains the true state, except for versioned entities (decision 9);
    8. archive remains allowed (it destroys nothing) and is how a subject is hidden from customers during a dispute;
    9. operational writes continue: sign-in bookkeeping, session and token records, credential rotation and membership or role changes, each of which is already an auditable access change (ADR-010). The refusal names the holds, to administrators and to the legal manager only.
    10. Refusals are recorded. A refused erasure, deletion or correction is an audit event, so the deferral of a data subject's request is evidenced.
    11. Versioned entities keep every before-image. An entity registered as versioned allows edits under a hold and stores each previous state (all fields, not only pii) in an append-only history. Such entities are marked in the catalog, and their history is subject to the hold: it is not purged while any hold covers the subject. The platform's own identity tables use the freeze (decision 7), not versioning.

    Roles and notification¶

    1. Two platform roles for privacy and legal, with privacy under legal. (A third, trust_safety_manager, for purging abusive accounts, is defined in decision 24.) Organizations place privacy inside legal, so the roles are separate in permission and nested in default assignment:
      • privacy_manager handles data subject requests: the catalog (read), access and portability exports, restriction (archive), and erasure and deletion requests, which are refused while a hold applies.
      • legal_manager places, changes and releases holds, decides whether a hold is disclosed, and holds every privacy_manager permission. A hold overrides erasure, so the role that can place one must be able to see the requests it defers. Both are platform-level roles in the SystemForge vocabulary (ADR-007), separate from platform administration, so that placing a hold or erasing a person is not available to everyone who can administer accounts. Permissions are named (privacy.request.manage, privacy.catalog.read, legal.hold.manage, legal.hold.disclose, audit.read) so a deployment whose privacy office sits outside legal can assign them differently. Holds and snapshots are readable only to legal_manager and to administrators with audit.read. The roles and permissions are part of the platform so every product gets the same generic capability; an application may extend them with its own roles or narrower permissions in its vocabulary, but may not weaken the platform's refusals (a hold still blocks erasure).

    2. Destructive and disclosing actions are requests. Erasure, deletion, hold release and disclosure run as a request with states requested, approved, executed or cancelled, so there is one audited path whatever the policy. A deployment sets a policy per action:

      • none: the requester's authority is enough and the request executes at once. This is the default, since many businesses start with one person who holds every role.
      • delay: the request executes after a configurable period, during which it can be cancelled and the stated notification is sent. This protects a single operator from a mistake without needing a second person.
      • approval: a second principal holding the permission, different from the requester, must approve. The approver cannot be the requester or the subject, and an unapproved request expires. The policy can be tightened without a code change as the business grows.

    3. A hold is marked internally, and notification is the legal manager's decision. The marker is visible to staff in admin tooling. Whether and how the person is told is set per hold: not at all, in the application (a notice the product renders from a claim or an API field), or out of band (a message the legal team sends). The default is not to notify, so a hold is not disclosed to a subject by accident. A subject whose erasure request is deferred is told only that the request is pending review, unless the legal manager has chosen to disclose.

    Snapshot storage¶

    1. The snapshot location is configurable. Two stores are supported behind one interface: the database (a sealed, encrypted record, the default and the one used at first, since the volume is expected to be small) and an S3-compatible object store, with the snapshot written as a file (Parquet for tabular exports) and referenced from the database by location and digest. Moving to the object store is a configuration change; existing snapshots remain readable through their recorded location. The same store, with the same manifest, holds data offloaded from the primary database under ADR-011, decision 25.
    2. Snapshots are personal data. They carry the classification of their contents, are encrypted at rest under keys managed as ADR-013 decides, are excluded from ordinary exports, and are removed only when every hold covering the subject has been released and the subject's own erasure has proceeded. The key hierarchy (per hold, per subject or platform-wide) is ADR-013's decision, not this one's.

    Enforcement in applications¶

    1. Applications reach their own data directly. SystemForge does not sit between a product and its database. Identity and organization data stays behind SystemForge's services, because it owns them; product data goes through the product's own access layer.
    2. Registration is mandatory and verified. A product annotates its Ent entities with classification (ADR-011, decision 7) and registers them in the catalog. dataclass.Verify runs at startup and fails in production, and warns in development, when a registered field has no classification or an entity with personal data has no subject key and eraser.
    3. SystemForge ships the enforcement as an Ent hook. A mutation hook consults the catalog and the holds covering the subject and refuses the writes that decision 7 forbids; for versioned entities it writes the before-image; for a subject marked purging it refuses new dependent writes (decision 22). A product installs it once on its client, so enforcement sits in the write path without a data service.
    4. A conformance test is provided. It fails in CI when a registered entity can be mutated without the hook installed, which catches a second client or a bulk path that skips it.
    5. Where tables share a database with SystemForge, a trigger or row-level-security policy may back the hook as defense in depth. This is optional and engine-specific, and it lives in the engine-specific packages, not in the portable core.
    6. What this does not stop is stated. Raw SQL, a bulk import script or a direct console session bypasses an ORM hook. The mitigations are operational and documented: lint rules against raw SQL in product code, guardsql for query paths, and control over who holds a database credential. The platform promises what a library can promise.

    Purge¶

    1. Purge planning is a walk of the catalog's relationships. Given a subject, the platform follows its registered entities and their edges, counts the rows each would remove, and classifies each dependent: it cascades (owned by the subject), it detaches (reference cleared), or it blocks. Anything that blocks produces the dependency report of ADR-011, decision 20: a row of another subject, a restrict edge with rows present, a row covered by a hold, an edge in the Ent schema that the catalog does not declare. The same walk is the dry run. The report holds entity names, counts and reasons and never row contents, and is shown only to a holder of the permission in decision 24.
    2. Execution runs through the hook, in the platform, not in the database. The plan is executed as ADR-011, decision 22 says: the subject is marked purging, new dependent writes are refused by the hook, dependents are removed leaf first in resumable batches, each batch is re-checked against the holds at the moment it runs (a hold placed mid-purge stops it with ErrHeld, and the rows removed so far are recorded), and the subject is removed last. Database cascades are not used on registered entities: they run inside the engine and skip the hook, so they would bypass hold checks, before-images and audit. Foreign keys stay RESTRICT.
    3. The catalog's relationships are verified against the schema. A registration check and the conformance test compare every edge of a registered Ent entity with the catalog and fail on an undeclared edge or a foreign key that is not RESTRICT. An entity added without a disposition cannot ship, as a field without a classification cannot.
    4. Purge is a separate permission and role. account.purge.plan runs the dry run and reads the dependency report; account.purge executes. They belong to a platform role, trust_safety_manager, separate from privacy_manager and legal_manager, because removing spam at volume is an abuse-handling duty and carries none of their authority over holds and data subject requests. Purge is a destructive request under decision 11, and its policy can differ by origin: expiry of provisional accounts is system-initiated and uses none, a single purge by an operator uses the deployment's policy, and a bulk purge by criteria (signup window, email domain, network range) always starts with a dry run and, above a configurable row threshold, requires approval where a second principal exists.

    Consequences¶

    • A hold is meaningful for mutable data. The sealed snapshot plus the freeze answers "what did it say then" without a history store on every table, and versioned entities cover records that must stay editable.
    • Several matters can cover one person. Release of one matter leaves the others in force, and the platform can answer "which holds cover this person" and "who is on this hold".
    • Placing and disclosing a hold are separate decisions held by a legal role, not by whoever administers accounts.
    • Products get governance by registering, not by building it. The catalog makes the platform's coverage inspectable and auditable.
    • The platform stays out of product data paths. No added hop, and a product can still split out. The cost is that enforcement is by convention, hook and test, and not by a mandatory interface.
    • Annotating entities is real work for each product; the startup check makes omissions loud.
    • The platform now models holds and custodians. It still does not model matters, deadlines, counsel or discovery; those stay in the legal team's systems, referenced by the external reference.
    • Purge is safe because it is planned and refused. A subject with dependents the catalog does not mark as owned cannot be removed, and the reason is reported, like a cloud provider's dependency violation.
    • Dependency edges are one more thing a product declares, and the verification step keeps that list from rotting.

    Alternatives considered¶

    • A single hold flag per subject. Rejected: it cannot express several matters, and releasing one would clear the rest.
    • All application data access through SystemForge. Rejected: makes the platform the data layer for every product, couples their schemas and deployment, adds latency, and still does not stop a credential holder.
    • Preserve against deletion only. Rejected: alteration defeats the purpose of a hold on mutable data.
    • Freeze every write under hold. Rejected: breaks sign-in, rotation and membership operations; the account must stay usable and secure.
    • Rely on ON DELETE CASCADE for purge. Rejected: cascades run inside the database and bypass the hook, so held rows could be removed, before- images skipped and no audit written.
    • Hard delete as the ordinary delete. Rejected: references to a deleted principal would break or cascade; the tombstone stays the default and purge is the narrow exception.
    • Tombstone everything, including spam. Rejected: it keeps a row, and an identifier, for each junk account at free-tier volumes, and retains data the platform has no reason to keep.
    • Versioning everywhere by default. Deferred: a history store on every classified table is heavy for a first version; kept as a per-entity option and recorded in the catalog.

    Open questions¶

    • How a data subject access request interacts with a hold the subject must not be told about (decision 12 defaults to "pending review").
    • The default delay period for the delay policy.
    • Whether a deployment with one principal can satisfy approval through an external approver (for example a named advisor with a scoped, temporary role), or must stay on none or delay.
    • The row threshold above which a bulk purge requires approval, and whether it differs for provisional accounts.
    • Whether a hold placed during a long purge should roll back the rows already removed (it cannot) or only stop and report, which is what decision 22 specifies.
